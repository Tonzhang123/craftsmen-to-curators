Abstract & Big Picture

What the paper is about

An exploratory multiple‑case study of how arts students learn to make films with generative AI — and how that reshapes their creative identity, cognition, and how we should assess them.

“

The study investigates the educational effectiveness of a 13‑week Project‑Based Learning (PBL) course involving 32 Master of Arts students for AI filmmaking. Grounded in Cognitive Load Theory and the Revised Bloom's Taxonomy, the authors theorize a structural transition in student creative identity: from traditional physical “craftsmen” to “curators of probabilities.”

Three core findings emerge: (1) students navigate cognitive friction and operational overhead — prompt fatigue, hallucination management, the “gacha” nature of AI; (2) adaptive collaborative workflows emerge; and (3) an assessment paradox appears, where product‑oriented rubrics fail to capture human–AI co‑creation. Students traverse the Revised Bloom's Taxonomy inversely, shifting from technical execution to aesthetic curation.

Functional division segments roles by pipeline stage (text‑to‑image, image‑to‑video, music). The One‑Man Crew (Group 3, top‑performing) eliminated translation loss but scaled poorly. Mixed division offered the most resilience.

The generalized 5‑phase GenAI filmmaking workflow

Phase 1

Pre‑Production & Concept Exploration

Scriptwriting, scene/character grouping, exploring target visual styles with reference images.

Phase 2

Static Asset Generation

Shared character/scene/storyboard libraries via T2I with intensive iteration; cleanup to minimize AI artifacts.

Phase 3

Video Generation

I2V models, several attempts per shot to obtain a few seconds of usable footage.

Phase 4

Audio Production

Music / voiceover to bypass lip‑syncing limitations.

Phase 5

Post‑Production & “De‑AI”

Cinematic re‑grounding — film grain, lens flares — to reduce the AI aesthetic and ground footage in traditional realism.

Comparative case analysis (High / Medium / Low tiers)

High · Group 3

One‑Man Crew + parallel‑universe pivot

Re‑designed narrative away from historical accuracy; generate‑and‑test pipeline dropped shots into the timeline immediately to catch gaps early. Scored top‑tier.

Medium · Group 4

Chronological division + clay style

Clay aesthetic forgave distortions and lowered rendering complexity. Split by character/era; fragmented audio tracks revealed separate asset generation.

Low · Group 2

Minimalist B&W line pivot

Reactive compromise after failing facial consistency. External reviewer scored it higher (51) than the instructor (41) — a textbook assessment paradox.

Theoretical Contribution

The Inverted Revised Bloom's Taxonomy

Traditional arts pedagogy climbs Bloom's ladder bottom‑up (mechanics → creation). With GenAI, students start at the top (Create) and reverse‑traverse downward as they diagnose friction. Click a tier to explore.

Tier 1 of 6 · Entry point

Create — Vision & Conceptualization

Students initiate the creative loop directly at the top tier, translating high‑level narrative concepts into visual prototypes via natural‑language prompts. The execution burden is offloaded to the model.

↓ Reverse‑traversed as friction appears
The Curatorial Shift

From craftsmen to “curators of probabilities”

CLT says traditional production imposes heavy load through manual frame‑by‑frame execution. GenAI offloads that physical load, reallocating cognition toward high‑level evaluative judgment. Friction forces active curation — through “De‑AI” re‑grounding, learners deploy aesthetic judgment to mask algorithmic artifacts. Human agency is not diminished; it relocates to curation.

Need for Process‑Trace Evaluation

Product‑only rubrics decouple from learning

Conventional PBL rubrics assume the final artifact reflects learning. The data challenge this: Group 2's clean line‑art film concealed severe operational friction. The authors call for Process‑Trace Evaluations that audit prompt logs, reflective diagnostics, and human–AI negotiation histories.

Exploratory Conceptual Framework

Three constructs, one sequential mechanism

Cognitive Friction → the Curatorial Shift → the Assessment Paradox. Friction from probabilistic systems drives learners to curate; that curation, in turn, hides learning inside artifacts — producing the paradox.

Pedagogical Guidelines (RQ3)

Four actionable design guidelines

To operationalize the Teacher–Student–AI triad and resolve the tension between stochasticity and human agency.

1

Prioritize Aesthetic Literacy & Spatial Prompting

Position GenAI as an execution engine while reinforcing cinematic theory, composition, and lighting. Train spatial prompting (layout, camera angles) to minimize hallucinations; screen award‑winning AI films to sharpen curatorial eyes.

2

Cultivate Affordance‑Based Adaptive Scripting

Replace rigid pre‑production scripts with continuous feedback loops among scriptwriting, rendering, and storyboarding. Teach students to adapt narratives to tool affordances — turning limitations into intentional creative choices.

3

Institutionalize Dual‑Track “Process‑Trace” Assessment

Assign 50% weight to Process (instructor: computational thinking, iteration rigor, De‑AI effort) and 50% to Product (external reviewer, fully blind). Resolves the paradox and recognizes unseen curatorial labor.

4

Account for Hidden Operational Costs

Provide shared access to subscription AI ecosystems for equity; explicitly budget course timelines for iterative, stochastic workflows — asset management, manual retouching, and post‑production refinement.

Limitations & Future Work

What the authors flag

  • Measured qualitatively. Cognitive friction was inferred from interviews and logs, not quantified. Future work should add NASA‑TLX or standardized Cognitive Load scales plus multi‑rater experimental panels.
  • Narrow sample. Restricted to Chinese‑speaking MA students in film/creative disciplines; tool choice skewed to regional platforms (Jimeng, Kling). Generalizability to STEM or international cohorts is untested.
  • Moving target. Rapid model updates may resolve specific bottlenecks (temporal inconsistency, lip‑sync), but the core cognitive challenge — curating probabilistic outputs — likely persists across GenAI generations.
Conclusion

Three contributions to learning‑technologies research

First, a selected case study of a 13‑week GenAI filmmaking course showing how a Teacher–Student–AI triad within PBL facilitates specific learning outcomes.

Second, an exploratory conceptual framework — grounded in Cognitive Load Theory and the Revised Bloom's Taxonomy — that conceptualizes the student transition from “craftsmen” to “curators of probabilities” via an inverse traversal of Bloom's.

Third, a systematic definition of the constructs, dimensions, and boundary conditions of Cognitive Friction, the Curatorial Shift, and the Assessment Paradox — culminating in dual‑track assessment guidelines for educators.
